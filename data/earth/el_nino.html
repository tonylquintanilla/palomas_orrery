1.What El Niño is

In a neutral year, the trade winds blow west along the equator, taking warm water toward Asia. The sea off Asia is about 8 °C warmer than in the eastern Pacific, where cold water rises from deeper levels off South America.

In an El Niño the trade winds weaken, and the warm water is pushed back east, toward the Americas.

In a La Niña the trade winds are even stronger than usual, pushing more warm water toward Asia, and more cold water rises off the Americas.

Think of the warm water sloshing from one side of the ocean to the other. The black box on the maps is the stretch of ocean NOAA watches, explained in the next step.

Sources: NOAA Ocean Service, What are El Niño and La Niña?; NOAA PMEL, What is El Niño?

2.Since 1950, in one line

The line follows a box of ocean along the equator that NOAA calls Niño 3.4.

More about the Niño 3.4 box

The name is not a latitude. Scientists first watched boxes numbered Niño 1 to Niño 4. Niño 3.4, named later, is the box between Niño 3 and Niño 4, overlapping both.

It runs from 5 degrees north of the equator to 5 degrees south, and from 120 to 170 degrees west.

Each sliver of the chart is one season: a three-month average. The seasons overlap, December–February, then January–March, and so on, so there are twelve to a year.

For each season NOAA asks two questions. First: how much warmer or cooler than usual is the water in the Niño 3.4 box? Second: how much warmer or cooler than usual are all the tropical oceans, taken together? The index is the first answer minus the second, then NOAA rescales it.

When the box has warmed more than the tropical oceans as a whole, the index is above zero. When it has warmed less, or cooled, the index is below zero. So the index can be above zero even when the box is a little cooler than usual, if the tropical oceans are cooler still. What counts is the difference.

The colors are NOAA’s classification. A season is red, El Niño, when it is one of at least five overlapping seasons in a row at +0.5 or more. It is blue, La Niña, in a run of at least five at −0.5 or less. Otherwise it is grey, neutral. So a sliver above zero can still be grey.

Why subtract? The scientists who proposed this index found that, measured the old way, global warming makes El Niño events “seem bigger than they are,” and La Niña events smaller. Taking away the warming of the tropical oceans as a whole takes that out of the measure.

For the technical reader

Vertical axis: NOAA’s Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), in degrees Celsius. Each point is a three-month running mean of the sea-surface temperature anomaly in the Niño 3.4 box (5°N–5°S, 120°W–170°W), minus the anomaly averaged over all tropical oceans (20°N–20°S), then rescaled to match the amplitude of the traditional index. Data: NOAA’s ERSST version 6. NOAA counts El Niño when the index is +0.5 or more for five overlapping seasons in a row, and La Niña at −0.5 or less.

On this index, the three strongest El Niño events are 1982–83, 1997–98 and 2015–16.

3.This year so far

NOAA issued its final La Niña advisory in April 2026, as neutral conditions emerged. By August–October the index stood at +1.97. NOAA’s newest values can still be revised.

The record on RONI, NOAA’s official index, is +2.40, from December–February 1982–83. It has not been passed yet.

This page’s record is NOAA’s three-month record on RONI. Other agencies report on another stretch of the Pacific, a single month, or a forecast, so a record reported elsewhere may not be this one.

Sources: NOAA CPC, RONI data file (above); NOAA ENSO discussion, 9 Apr 2026; Japan Meteorological Agency, El Niño Outlook (10 Nov 2026); WMO press conference (6 Nov 2026)

5.Why the whole planet feels it

During La Niña, the tropical Pacific stores extra heat at depth. During El Niño that heat goes out into the air, warming the Pacific and then the world.

Global average temperature typically follows the Pacific’s warming by two to three months.

El Niño’s heat now lands on a warmer world. Whether global warming is also making El Niño events stronger is still being studied.
