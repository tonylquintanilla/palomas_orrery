## Four decisions

1. **C/2025 K1: the orrery is drawing the wrong comet today.** Its
   entry asks JPL for record 90004912. JPL has renumbered: that number
   now holds a different comet, PANSTARRS (C/2025 B1). The comet and
   its three pieces are now records 90004925 to 90004928. The
   designation alone does not pick one record either: asking for
   "C/2025 K1" returns all four pieces. Two ways to serve it:
   - pin the new record, 90004925 (it can change hands again, as this
     one just did), or
   - ask by JPL's permanent object number for the comet, 1004075,
     which returned exactly one record, the right one. The skill has no
     rule yet for a comet whose designation matches its own pieces.
2. **BepiColombo's arrival date.** ESA's pages give three answers:
   "late 2025" (its science site, out of date), "December 2026" (its
   mission page), and "enter orbit around Mercury on 21 November 2026"
   (its arrival news, updated September 3). Proposed: "in November
   2026". That sentence goes out of date in about four weeks.
3. **Juno: is it still flying?** NASA's Juno page says it explores
   Jupiter "through September 2025" and then burns up in Jupiter's
   atmosphere. JPL's sheet, revised September 30, 2026, still lists
   "End of mission: Sep , 2025" but carries a planned path to
   September 2, 2028. The words below make no claim either way. Should
   the website draw that planned part, dashed, as it would for any
   other spacecraft?
4. **The word "Hyperbolic".** Most of the orrery's comet lines open
   with "Hyperbolic." JPL does class all of them as hyperbolic except
   fragment C. But a visitor does not know the word, and "an open
   path" can read as "never comes back", which is not always true for
   a comet from the Oort cloud. Proposed: drop it, and let the drawn
   path show it. Keep it in plain words instead?

### Spacecraft (12)

- **Voyager 1**
  - Now: Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 is the farthest spacecraft from
    Earth.
  - Proposed: Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 is the farthest spacecraft
    from Earth. In 2012 it became the first spacecraft to reach
    interstellar space.
  - Why: NASA's Voyager 1 page. Link unchanged (NASA's Voyager page).
- **Voyager 2**
  - Now: Launched in 1977, Voyager 2 explored all four giant planets.
  - Proposed: Launched in 1977, Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to
    have visited all four giant planets, and the only one to visit
    Uranus and Neptune.
- **New Horizons**
  - Now: New Horizons flew past Pluto in 2015 and continues into the
    Kuiper Belt.
  - Proposed: New Horizons flew past Pluto in July 2015 and past
    Arrokoth on January 1, 2019. It is still travelling outward
    through the Kuiper Belt.
  - Link: NASA's New Horizons page (the old address redirects there).
- **Juno**
  - Now: Juno studies Jupiter's atmosphere and magnetosphere.
  - Proposed: NASA's Juno has orbited Jupiter since July 2016, studying
    its interior, its atmosphere and the region its magnetic field
    controls, the magnetosphere.
  - Why: NASA's page. Nothing about when it ends: decision 3. Link:
    NASA's Juno page (the old address redirects there).
- **Lucy**
  - Now: Exploring Trojan asteroids around Jupiter.
  - Proposed: NASA's Lucy, launched in October 2021, is on its way to
    the Trojan asteroids, which share an orbit around the Sun with
    Jupiter.
  - Link: NASA's Lucy page (the old address redirects there).
- **JUICE**
  - Now: JUICE (Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer) is an ESA mission to study
    Jupiter and its moons.
  - Proposed: ESA's Juice (Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer), launched in
    April 2023, reaches Jupiter in July 2031. It will study the planet
    and its three large moons that hold oceans: Ganymede, Callisto and
    Europa.
  - Link: ESA's Juice page, not an ESA search page.
- **Psyche** (the spacecraft)
  - Now: The NASA Psyche mission is a journey to metal-rich asteroid
    16 Psyche, located in the main asteroid belt. Arrival phase ends
    June 2029.
  - Proposed: NASA's Psyche spacecraft, launched in October 2023, is on
    its way to the metal-rich asteroid 16 Psyche in the main asteroid
    belt. It begins exploring the asteroid by August 2029.
  - Why: NASA gives August 2029; no page says June. JPL's path for it
    now stops on February 11, 2029, so the drawing ends before it
    arrives. Link: NASA's Psyche page, not the mission team's site.
- **Clipper** (Europa Clipper)
  - Now: Europa Clipper will conduct detailed reconnaissance of
    Jupiter's moon Europa.
  - Proposed: NASA's Europa Clipper, launched in October 2024, reaches
    Jupiter in April 2030. It will fly past Jupiter's moon Europa 49
    times, to learn whether the ocean thought to lie under its ice
    could support life.
  - Link: NASA's Europa Clipper page (the old address redirects there).
- **BepiColombo**
  - Now: BepiColombo is the joint ESA/JAXA mission to study Mercury,
    arriving in 2025.
  - Proposed: BepiColombo, led by ESA with Japan's space agency JAXA,
    launched in October 2018 to study Mercury. It enters orbit around
    Mercury in November 2026.
  - Why: it did not arrive in 2025; the month is decision 2. Link:
    ESA's BepiColombo mission page, not its science site, which still
    says late 2025.
- **SolO** (Solar Orbiter)
  - Now: Solar Orbiter ("SolO"), an ESA/NASA solar probe mission
  - Proposed: Solar Orbiter, an ESA mission with a NASA contribution,
    launched in February 2020 to study the Sun up close. It has taken
    the first close-up pictures of the Sun's poles.
  - Link: ESA's Solar Orbiter page, not Wikipedia (an ESA mission).
- **Parker** (Parker Solar Probe)
  - Now: The Parker Solar Probe mission is to study the outer corona of
    the Sun.
  - Proposed: NASA's Parker Solar Probe, launched in August 2018,
    became the first spacecraft to fly through the corona, the Sun's
    upper atmosphere, in 2021. It studies how the corona is heated and
    what speeds up the solar wind.
  - Why: NASA's page gives two different closest distances, so no
    distance. Link: NASA's Parker page (the old address redirects
    there).
- **OSIRISAPE** (OSIRIS-APEX)
  - Now: OSIRIS-APEX is NASA's mission to study asteroid Apophis.
  - Proposed: OSIRIS-APEX, formerly OSIRIS-REx, is on its way to
    asteroid Apophis. It reaches Apophis in June 2029, soon after the
    asteroid passes close to Earth in April 2029.
  - Link: NASA's OSIRIS-APEX mission page, not a list of blog posts.

### Comets (14)

JPL's name is given first for each. A comet on this list that goes
"in the opposite direction to the planets" has a tilt over 90 degrees
in JPL's database.

- **Tsuchinshan** -- JPL: Tsuchinshan-ATLAS (C/2023 A3)
  - Now: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. Tsuchinshan-ATLAS is a new comet
    discovered in 2023, expected to become bright in 2024.
  - Proposed: Discovered in January 2023, it became known as the Great
    Comet of 2024, visible to the naked eye from late September 2024.
    It goes around the Sun in the opposite direction to the planets.
- **ATLAS** -- JPL: ATLAS (C/2024 G3)
  - Now: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. The Great Comet of 2025. Comet C/2024
    G3 (ATLAS) created quite a buzz in the Southern Hemisphere!
  - Proposed: Discovered in April 2024 and called the Great Comet of
    2025, the brightest comet of that year. After passing the Sun it
    was clearly visible to the naked eye from the Southern Hemisphere.
    It goes around the Sun in the opposite direction to the planets.
- **West** -- JPL: West (C/1975 V1-A)
  - Now: Hyperbolic. Notable for its bright and impressive tail.
  - Proposed: One of the brightest objects to pass through the inner
    solar system in 1976. As it passed close to the Sun its nucleus
    split into four pieces; the path drawn is piece A's, the one JPL
    tracks.
  - Why: no page opened says "bright tail"; Wikipedia's words instead.
- **McNaught** -- JPL: McNaught (C/2006 P1)
  - Now: Hyperbolic. Known as the Great Comet of 2007. Retrograde
    (left-handed) orbit.
  - Proposed: Discovered in August 2006 by Robert McNaught and called
    the Great Comet of 2007, it was the brightest comet in over 40
    years, easily seen with the naked eye from the Southern Hemisphere
    in January and February 2007.
  - Why: not retrograde (above). Link: Wikipedia's "C/2006 P1
    (McNaught)", not NASA's general comet page.
- **C/2025_K1** -- JPL: ATLAS (C/2025 K1)
  - Now: Retrograde. Hyperbolic (e=1.00026). Perihelion Oct 8, 2025 at
    0.33 AU. Nucleus fragmented ~Oct 16 into at least 4 pieces (Hubble,
    published Icarus Feb 2026). This record is Fragment A (main body,
    non-gravitational forces modeled). See K1-B, K1-C, K1-D. Fragment
    separation at perihelion is a backward-extrapolation artifact
    (comet was still intact Oct 8).
  - Proposed: A comet that broke apart about a month after passing
    closest to the Sun: in November 2025 NASA's Hubble Space Telescope
    caught it splitting into at least four pieces. This path is the
    comet's own, worked out from sightings before and after the
    break-up. It goes around the Sun in the opposite direction to the
    planets.
  - Why: NASA's Hubble pages say it was intact days before Hubble's
    pictures of November 8 to 10, and the paper came out in March
    2026. No page calls this record fragment A. Which record: decision
    1. Link: NASA's Hubble page on the break-up; the old link did not
    answer.
  - The commented-out line above the entry (id 'C/2025 K1') would now
    return four records, not one.
- **C/2025_K1-B** -- JPL: ATLAS (C/2025 K1-B)
  - Now: Fragment B of comet C/2025 K1 (ATLAS). Hyperbolic
    (e=1.00203). Breakup observed by Hubble ~Oct 16, 2025, 8 days
    post-perihelion. Escaping the solar system. Positions before ~Oct
    16 are backward-extrapolated (comet was still intact at
    perihelion).
  - Proposed: Piece B of comet C/2025 K1 (ATLAS), one of at least four
    pieces Hubble saw in November 2025. Its path is worked out from
    sightings after the break-up, so the part drawn before then is
    traced backwards. It goes around the Sun in the opposite direction
    to the planets.
  - Link (all three pieces): NASA's Hubble page on the break-up.
- **C/2025_K1-C** -- JPL: ATLAS (C/2025 K1-C)
  - Now: Fragment C - the stubborn one. Elliptical (e=0.99999, still
    bound!). While siblings escape, C remains gravitationally bound.
    Return period ~13 million years. Positions before ~Oct 16 are
    backward-extrapolated (comet was still intact at perihelion).
  - Proposed: Piece C of comet C/2025 K1 (ATLAS), one of at least four
    pieces Hubble saw in November 2025. Its path is worked out from
    sightings after the break-up, so the part drawn before then is
    traced backwards. It goes around the Sun in the opposite direction
    to the planets.
  - Why: JPL's figure falls short of an open path by less than JPL's
    own uncertainty in it, so whether C will come back cannot be told.
- **C/2025_K1-D** -- JPL: ATLAS (C/2025 K1-D)
  - Now: Fragment D of comet C/2025 K1 (ATLAS). Hyperbolic (e=1.00246,
    most divergent). Shortest observation arc (33 days). Escaping the
    solar system. Positions before ~Oct 16 are backward-extrapolated.
  - Proposed: Piece D of comet C/2025 K1 (ATLAS), one of at least four
    pieces Hubble saw in November 2025. It was followed for only 33
    days, the shortest of the four, so the part drawn before the
    break-up is traced backwards. It goes around the Sun in the
    opposite direction to the planets.
- **PANSTARRS** -- JPL: PANSTARRS (C/2025 R3)
  - Now: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. Oort Cloud origin. Inbound orbit
    ~170,000 yr ellipse; Jupiter gravitational slingshot this pass
    pushed e > 1.0 -- comet now exceeds solar escape velocity. One-way
    ejection. Perihelion: Apr 19, 2026 at 0.499 AU. Earth closest
    approach: Apr 26 at 0.489 AU. Gas-rich, dust-poor. Ion tail
    exceeded 10 deg by Apr 8; naked eye (mag 5.1) Apr 11.
  - Proposed: A comet from the Oort cloud, discovered in September
    2025. On April 11, 2026 it was seen with the naked eye. It goes
    around the Sun in the opposite direction to the planets.
  - Why: no page opened states the Jupiter push, the old orbit or
    "gas-rich"; the tail figure disagrees with Wikipedia's. Link:
    Wikipedia's "C/2025 R3 (PanSTARRS)"; the old link was blocked.
- **Borisov** -- JPL: Borisov (C/2025 V1)
  - Now: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. Discovered 11-2-2025. Most likely
    originated from the Oort Cloud.
  - Proposed: A comet from the Oort cloud. It goes around the Sun in
    the opposite direction to the planets.
  - Why: no page opened gives the discovery date. Link: JPL's database
    page; the old link did not answer.
- **1I/Oumuamua** -- JPL: 'Oumuamua (A/2017 U1)
  - Now: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. First known interstellar object
    detected passing through the Solar System. Retrograde
    (left-handed) orbit.
  - Proposed: The first known object from another star system seen
    passing through ours, discovered in October 2017 by the University
    of Hawaii's Pan-STARRS1 telescope. It passes the Sun in the
    opposite direction to the planets.
  - JPL's name begins with a plain apostrophe; it has no "1I/" in it,
    and JPL lists it as an asteroid, not a comet.
- **2I/Borisov** -- JPL: Borisov (C/2019 Q4)
  - Now: Hyperbolic. The second interstellar object detected, after
    '1I/Oumuamua.
  - Proposed: The first comet known to come from another star system,
    and only the second visitor of any kind, after 'Oumuamua.
    Discovered by Gennady Borisov in August 2019.
  - Why: NASA gives August 30, Wikipedia August 29 (by universal
    time), so the month only.
- **3I/ATLAS** -- JPL: ATLAS (C/2025 N1)
  - Now: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. Third known interstellar object
    detected passing through the Solar System. Retrograde
    (left-handed) orbit.
  - Proposed: The third known object from outside our solar system,
    first reported on July 1, 2025 by the NASA-funded ATLAS survey

    the planets.
- **Wierzchos** -- JPL: Wierzchos (C/2024 E1)
  - Now: CO2-driven Oort Cloud comet. Perihelion Jan 20, 2026 at 0.56
    AU. Multiple dust tails post-perihelion.
  - Proposed: A comet from the Oort cloud, discovered in March 2024 by

    After passing the Sun it showed three dust tails.
  - Link: NASA's picture page for it (the old APOD address redirects
