2. Does a citable table or document exist giving position uncertainty by class of body?
Plain answer: No. No single citable table or document gives position uncertainties across planets, the major moons of each planet, and spacecraft.
Instead, professional producers publish accuracy information separately by ephemeris (a mathematical table or file giving positions over time):
 * Planets and Earth's Moon: Documented in the release papers for JPL's Development Ephemeris (DE) series. Your current planetary numbers come from the 2014 DE430 report (Folkner et al. 2014). Note that Horizons now uses DE441 (a long-span twin of DE440), documented in Park et al. (2021), The Astronomical Journal, 161:105 (JPL PDF). Even that paper does not present a single summary table of 3D position uncertainties in kilometres; instead, Section 5.1 ("Residuals", p. 13) reports how closely the ephemeris fits the underlying tracking data (for example, recent lunar laser-ranging residuals of "about 1.3 cm", Mercury MESSENGER radio-range residuals of "about 0.7 m", Jupiter Juno range residuals of "about 13m", Saturn Cassini range residuals of "about 3 m", and Pluto stellar occultation residuals of "about 8 mas in R.A. and about 11 mas in decl.").
 * Major moons: Documented planet by planet in separate satellite-ephemeris papers (such as MAR099 for Mars, SAT441 for Saturn, and PLU060 for Pluto)—and as you saw with JUP365, not every current satellite fit has a published paper.
 * Spacecraft: No master table exists; accuracy is specific to each mission and flight phase (see Question 5).

5. Interplanetary spacecraft: is there a class-wide uncertainty, or must each mission be checked?
Plain answer: No class-wide uncertainty figure exists for interplanetary spacecraft. Position uncertainty varies by orders of magnitude depending on the spacecraft's distance from Earth, how often NASA's Deep Space Network (DSN) tracks it, what forces act on it (thruster firings, solar radiation pressure, thermal recoil), and whether the Horizons file is a past reconstruction or a future prediction. Each mission must be checked on its own.
Keeping your three cases apart, based on the Horizons System Manual, section "Spacecraft Trajectories in Horizons" (JPL Horizons Manual):
 * A craft being tracked now (active mission, reconstructed past arc): Navigation teams fit a trajectory to radio tracking data—chiefly two-way radio range (round-trip signal travel time), Doppler (frequency shift giving line-of-sight speed), and Delta-DOR (a two-station radio interferometry measurement giving sky angle). Horizons notes that a spacecraft file "typically includes past intervals precisely fit to tracking data, with predictions over future intervals", and warns that "Spacecraft trajectory data is not being relayed in 'real-time'" and "may only be updated weekly or monthly". For well-tracked inner-planet orbiters, DSN range noise is on the order of meters—for example, "DSN range noise of 3 m (1-sigma)" (Burt et al., AIAA 2022)—and total reconstructed position accuracy can be meters to tens of meters, whereas distant cruise craft (like Voyager or New Horizons) have much larger transverse (side-to-side) uncertainties because a tiny angular error spans tens to hundreds of kilometres at outer-planet distances.
 * A mission that has ended, at its last contact (final historical reconstruction): Horizons notes that "A flight project might provide an initial planning trajectory prior to launch and a final historical trajectory after end of mission." At its last contact, the position comes from that final post-flight reconstruction—generally the best-known part of the mission—with one documented catch: older archival trajectories were fit against older planetary ephemerides (like DE200), so "multi-km offsets can appear if output is instead requested relative to a modern target-body trajectory" (JPL Horizons Manual). (Also, if a public page shows an ended craft like Pioneer 10 today, decades after last contact, that is no longer a tracked position—it is an unmonitored ballistic extrapolation.)
 * A predicted path (future arc): Much less certain, and subject to sudden obsolescence if the mission fires its thrusters. Horizons warns: "maneuvers and mission planning changes can occur without notification, making a trajectory obsolete after some point in time." It instructs users: "Always check the revision date in the upper left corner of the Horizons spacecraft data-sheet" (JPL Horizons Manual).
Where to look up each mission: Start with the Horizons spacecraft data-sheet header and comments (which name the navigation team and the last tracking-data cutoff date—for instance, New Horizons' data-sheet lists its KinetX navigation cutoff date directly in Horizons). For published accuracy numbers, check the mission's archive at JPL's NAIF (Navigation and Ancillary Information Facility, naif.jpl.nasa.gov) and the mission's navigation papers (many JPL mission navigation reports are archived in JPL's DESCANSO series, e.g., Thornton & Border, DESCANSO Monograph 1).
6. Comets and interstellar objects not observed for years or decades
Plain answer: Horizons does provide a formal uncertainty for a comet's or interstellar object's present position whenever JPL's Small-Body Database stores a covariance matrix (the statistical error table from the orbit fit) for that object. When Horizons returns n.a. (as you saw for Halley, Ikeya-Seki, and Bennu), it is either because "no covariance in the database" exists for that historical orbit solution (JPL Horizons Manual, "Statistical Uncertainties") or—in the case of a spacecraft-visited target like Bennu—because the query selected a fixed historical mission-target "major body" trajectory rather than the live small-body database solution (JPL Horizons Manual, "Major Body").
 * How fast the uncertainty grows: Once observations stop, position uncertainty grows at least linearly with time along the orbit (a tiny uncertainty in orbital speed or period adds up every day), and quadratically (with time squared) or faster whenever non-gravitational forces—rocket-like pushes from outgassing ice on comets, or uneven thermal radiation (the Yarkovsky effect) on small asteroids—or close planetary encounters are involved. For example, Chesley et al. (2014, Icarus 235:5, p. 6, PDF) note that non-gravitational perturbation "causes a runoff in orbital anomaly that accumulates quadratically with time". For 1I/'Oumuamua, astronomers found "a 30-σ significant detection of nongravitational acceleration" over just a 2.5-month observation arc (Jewitt & Seligman 2023, arXiv:2304.00568, Section 2.4)—and because we do not know whether or when that acceleration shut off after it faded from view, any present-day position is heavily model-dependent.
 * Is printing a present distance defensible? Printing whole kilometres (or many decimal places) for a comet or interstellar object unobserved for years or decades is not defensible. Even Horizons' own propagated numbers can be too small: the manual warns that because it projects uncertainties using a linearized approximation, "the specific numbers output can tend to understate orbit uncertainty knowledge" far from the observation epoch (JPL Horizons Manual, "Close-Approach Tables"). Printing a heavily rounded distance (e.g., to 2 or 3 significant figures, or rounded to match the propagated 3-sigma uncertainty) is defensible only if you explicitly label it as an extrapolated/model estimate and state when the object was last observed.
7. The alternative: fix a lay-friendly ceiling (e.g., 4 significant figures)
Plain answer: Yes—choosing a fixed, human-scale precision (such as 3 or 4 significant figures) and verifying that the true uncertainty is smaller than the last printed digit is essentially how printed almanacs and NASA's public-facing pages work.
For example, the Air Almanac fixes its tables to "a precision of 1' of arc and 1s of time" to match air-navigation needs (American Practical Navigator Glossary), and NASA's public Planetary Fact Sheets round distances to a handful of readable digits rather than printing full ephemeris precision.
For Paloma's Orrery, a 4-significant-figure ceiling (0.3200 AU and 47.87 million km, rather than 0.32001664 AU and 47,873,807 km) is very practical: 1 part in 10,000 is ~15,000 km at 1 AU and ~1.5 million km at Pluto, so every planet, every major moon, and almost every tracked spacecraft easily clears that bar without requiring you to hunt down whether Io's ephemeris is 10 km or 15 km.
Its weaknesses:
 * It still needs an uncertainty check for poorly known bodies: A long-unobserved comet, a short-arc asteroid, an unobserved interstellar object, or an orphaned spacecraft prediction can easily have a real uncertainty larger than 1 part in 1,000 (0.1%). For those bodies, 4 significant figures would still overstate what is known, so you still need a rule to round further (or switch to "extrapolated / approximate") when uncertainty exceeds the 4th digit.
 * It hides the real contrast in human knowledge: A visitor cannot tell from 4-sig-fig numbers alone that we know the Moon's distance to centimetres, Mars's to meters, and Neptune's to thousands of kilometres. If showing how well we know is part of your educational goal, you would want to pair the clean 4-sig-fig distance with a short plain-language note (such as "known to within ~1 km" or "known to ~10,000 km").
 * Both units must match: If you print both AU and kilometres, both must be rounded to the same 4 significant figures—never 4 digits in AU next to 8 digits in km.
8. Precision, accuracy, and uncertainty; formal uncertainty vs. true error; 1-sigma vs. 3-sigma
Plain answer: Your working definitions of precision and uncertainty are sound, with two important distinctions that will make your public wording even more accurate.
 * Precision, accuracy, and uncertainty:
   * Precision is how finely a number is expressed (the number of digits printed), or how tightly repeated measurements agree with each other.
   * Accuracy is how close the stated number is to the unknown true value. The GUM defines accuracy as "closeness of the agreement between the result of a measurement and a true value of the measurand" and notes that "'Accuracy' is a qualitative concept. The term precision should not be used for 'accuracy'" (JCGM 100:2008, Annex B.2.14, p. 35).
   * Uncertainty is the quantified doubt—the spread of values reasonably consistent with the observations and dynamical model (JCGM 100:2008, Section 2.2.3, p. 2). Using uncertainty to decide how much precision (how many digits) to print is exactly right.
 * Formal uncertainty vs. true error:
   * A formal uncertainty is the statistical error bar calculated by an orbit-fitting program assuming that measurement errors are purely random and the physics model is complete.
   * The true error (how far off the ephemeris actually is from reality) is often several times larger than the formal uncertainty, because real observations contain systematic biases (star-catalog errors, clock offsets, unmodeled outgassing, or thruster leaks).
   * Professional producers explicitly warn about this gap:
     * JPL Horizons Manual, "Statistical Uncertainties" (JPL Horizons): "If there are systematic biases (such as measurement timing, reduction, or star-catalog errors), results can be optimistic."
     * Minor Planet Center, Uncertainty Parameter U (MPC U-Value): multiplies formal errors by "3 is a empirical factor to make the formal errors more closely model reality".
     * Fayolle et al. (2024), Icarus, Section 6.2.1 (arXiv:2405.00562): "Based on previous radio-science estimations of e.g. natural bodies' gravity fields and rotations, ratios of about 10 can be expected" (referring to the ratio of true error to formal uncertainty).
 * 1-sigma vs. 3-sigma — which should a public page use, and say?
   * 1-sigma (one standard deviation, the standard measure of spread around an average) covers only about 68% of a bell-curve distribution—meaning nearly one-third of the time, the true value lies outside 1-sigma even with zero systematic error.
   * 3-sigma (three standard deviations) covers about 99.7% under ideal random errors—as Horizons states in "Statistical Uncertainties" (JPL Horizons), "There is a 99.7% chance the actual value is within given bounds". This also aligns with the GUM's expanded uncertainty practice, which multiplies the standard uncertainty by a coverage factor "usually in the range 2 to 3" (JCGM 100:2008, Section 6.3.1, p. 24).
   * What to use and say: Your choice to use 3-sigma is the right call for a public page—it protects lay readers from the 1-in-3 miss rate of 1-sigma and provides a modest cushion against formal-error optimism. In your public note, say something like "rounded to match the published 3-sigma (99.7%) formal uncertainty" rather than claiming every printed digit is guaranteed exact, so you stay honest about the difference between a formal bound and true error.
