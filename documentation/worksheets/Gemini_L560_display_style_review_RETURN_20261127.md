4. Evaluation of the style you are weighing
Plain answer: Your proposed style is clean, honest, and lay-friendly, with three small friction points: trailing zeros below 1 million km, awkward leading zeros for moons in au, and occasional 1-digit rounding disagreements between the two units.
 * a. "47.87 million km" vs "47,870,000 km" vs scientific notation: "47.87 million km" is the right choice for a public site. As NIST SP 811, Sec. 7.9 points out, trailing zeros in plain integers ("1200 m") are ambiguous—readers cannot tell whether they are significant or placeholders. Word scaling ("47.87 million km", "25.80 billion km") removes placeholder zeros without forcing scientific notation (4.787 × 10⁷ km), and matches NASA Science and AMNH. Note that below 1 million km, "384,400 km" still carries two trailing zeros; that is a reasonable compromise for lay readability as long as your page states the four-significant-figure rule once.
 * b. Small values in au (Moon = 0.002570 au): Reputable public sources routinely switch units by scale—using km (and miles) alone for planet-to-moon or close-flyby distances (e.g., "8,400 miles (13,600 kilometers)" on NASA Science) and introducing au at interplanetary scales. NIST SP 811, Sec. 7.9 likewise recommends keeping numbers "between 0.1 and 1000" for readability. For a lay visitor, 0.002570 au is hard to parse and adds little intuition next to 384,400 km. Dropping au below a clear threshold (such as < 0.01 au, or for planet-centered moons) is gentler on the reader than forcing both units everywhere.
 * c. Can the two 4-significant-figure numbers disagree? Yes. Because 1\text{ au} = 149.5978707\text{ million km}, rounding the exact value independently to 4 significant figures in each unit can produce a pair that disagrees by 1 in the last digit if a reader multiplies them back. For example, 0.32004 au (47,877,303 km) rounds to 0.3200 au and 47.88 million km, yet 0.3200 × 149.6 gives 47.87. As NIST SP 811, Sec. B.7.2 notes, converting between units always involves a trade-off between losing information and implying extra precision. Careful sources avoid compounding the error by always rounding each unit directly from the unrounded source number (never converting an already-rounded display number) and noting once that the two units are rounded independently.
 * d. "AU" vs "au": The formal international standard is lowercase au—adopted by IAU 2012 Resolution B2, NIST SP 330, Table 8, JPL Solar System Dynamics ("now denoted with lower case letters (au)"), AAS Journals, MNRAS, and newer NASA Science pages ("1.4 au"). Uppercase AU remains common on legacy NASA and museum pages and is slightly more immediately recognizable to lay readers. If you define au once on the page ("au = astronomical unit"), au is the cleaner, standards-aligned choice.
 * e. Thousands separators and "billion" outside the U.S.: Many non-English countries use a comma as a decimal point (384,400 can look like 384.4), which is why NIST SP 811, Sec. 10.5.3 prefers thin spaces (384 400). Similarly, "billion" means 10^9 throughout the English-speaking world but 10^{12} in Spanish, French, and German—why NIST SP 811, Sec. 7.10.3 cautions against it in international technical prose. On an English-language public site, commas and "billion" (10^9) are normal (NASA uses both); adding one line in your precision note ("billion = 1,000 million") prevents any misunderstanding.
 * f. Omitting miles: For a predominantly U.S. lay audience, omitting miles is a real loss of intuition. Most U.S. visitors carry mental anchors in miles ("93 million miles to the Sun," "240,000 miles to the Moon"), which is why NASA Science, AMNH, and Adler include them. Printing three units at once (au, km, mi) would clutter the box, so keeping au and km as the default is a defensible editorial choice—or a simple km/mi toggle (like NASA's Voyager page) would serve U.S. readers without adding clutter.
5. Spacecraft: one citable source and the three trajectory regimes
Plain answer: Yes—for the fitted (reconstructed) part of a deep-space spacecraft's trajectory, Thornton & Border (JPL DESCANSO Monograph 1) supports radiometric range accuracy at the few-meter level, far finer than 1 part in 10,000 (which is ~15,000 km at 1 au and ~38 km at the Moon). That source does not cover future predictions or un-tracked post-contact drifts.
 * The source: Catherine L. Thornton and James S. Border, Radiometric Tracking Techniques for Deep-Space Navigation, JPL DESCANSO Monograph Series, Monograph 1 (October 2000; Wiley 2003), Chapter 3 (PDF) and complete monograph (PDF).
   * On DSN two-way ranging hardware limits (Chapter 3, Sec. 3.5): "This instrument bias is about 2 m."
   * On achieved deep-space ranging (Chapter 3, Sec. 3.6, citing Ulysses near Jupiter): "range accuracies of a few meters were achievable"
   * On how orbit-determination fits are checked (Chapter 2, Sec. 2.1): formal uncertainties come from the least-squares fit, validated against "postfit residuals" and subsequent tracking data.
   * Note: Radiometric range measures line-of-sight distance from Earth directly to meters; full 3D position (needed for Sun-centered distance) also depends on cross-track angular accuracy, which for deep-space navigation is typically kilometers to tens of kilometers—still orders of magnitude better than 1 part in 10,000 at interplanetary scales (for historical context, JPL's The Evolution of Deep Space Navigation: 1962–1989 cites Viking Mars delivery accuracies of "25 km and 37 km").
 * Keeping the three regimes apart:
   * Trajectory fitted to tracking data (reconstructed): Covered by Thornton & Border as a class for DSN-tracked interplanetary spacecraft. Horizons data sheets mark this exact boundary—for example, Mars Odyssey states "Fit to data through 2026-Oct-29, prediction after that." Inside the fitted span, 4 significant figures is comfortably supported.
   * Ended mission at its last contact: Up to the last tracking pass, the trajectory is reconstructed and shares the same high precision. After last contact, no new tracking data exist; any later position is an unverified coasting extrapolation (subject to unmodeled outgassing, thermal recoil, or leaks). Thornton & Border does not certify 4 significant figures indefinitely past last contact.
   * Predicted path (future dates): Prediction uncertainty grows over time and can jump abruptly if a planned maneuver is altered or cancelled—as the Mars Odyssey Horizons sheet cautions ("DO NOT use for flight operations" on a preliminary maneuver model). Thornton & Border does not cover arbitrary future predictions; under your Rule 3, predicted spans need their own mission-specific bound (or should be left without a distance figure beyond the fitted/bounded span).
6. Anything in the rule and style together that could mislead or look wrong to a professional
 * Center-to-center vs. surface distance: JPL Horizons returns "bodycentric" (center-to-center) distances by default (Horizons Manual). For a planet from the Sun, that distinction is invisible at 4 significant figures. For a spacecraft orbiting Mars or the Moon, however, a lay visitor often reads "distance from Mars" as altitude above the surface rather than distance from the planet's center—a difference of ~3,390 km at Mars. Stating "center-to-center" once in the precision note (or in the label for close orbiters) prevents that misreading.
 * Archived spacecraft trajectories vs. modern planetary ephemerides: The Horizons Manual warns that archived spacecraft trajectories may be tied to older planetary ephemerides (e.g., DE200 rather than DE441), which can create "multi-km offsets" if compared against a modern target-body trajectory. At interplanetary distances this is invisible in 4 significant figures, but for a close planetary orbiter or flyby it can shift the 3rd or 4th digit.
 * Rule 2 ("three sigma") vs. what Horizons actually supplies: "Sigma" (standard deviation) measures the statistical spread of an error; three sigma covers ~99.7% of a normal error distribution. Horizons publishes formal uncertainties for asteroids and comets, but does not output a sigma column for planets or spacecraft. Applying Rule 2 honestly to spacecraft means tying the printed number to the fitted date span in each Horizons data sheet rather than assuming every date in Horizons is equally well-known.
 * Trailing zeros below 1 million km and independent-unit rounding: As noted in Question 4, "384,400 km" visually looks like 6 digits unless the reader sees your editorial-rounding note, and independently rounding au and km to 4 significant figures can differ by 1 in the last digit. A single plain-English sentence on the page—stating that distances are center-to-center, rounded independently in each unit to at most four significant figures for readability, with 1 billion = 1,000 million—resolves all of these cleanly.
