Tony, I would adopt 0.3200 au from the Sun (47.87 million km). For a moon or a spacecraft close to the selected body, I would reverse the order and lead with kilometres. Hayden’s Digital Universe Guide gives you a planetarium precedent for writing millions and billions this way. The International Astronomical Union gives you a standard for au. The four-figure ceiling remains Paloma’s editorial choice. 

One detail in your examples shows why the conversion rule matters. If 172.5 au and 25.80 billion km are meant as a pair, they can both be right. But converting the already rounded 172.5 gives 25.81 billion km. The only way to decide which kilometres belong on the page is to go back to the original distance.

1. Is there a public standard?

None found that governs the entire label. There is published guidance on the unit symbol, digit grouping, conversion and the wording of large numbers. I found no authority that prescribes a number of significant figures for a public solar-system display.

NASA. The closest editorial match is the NASA Glenn Content Guide, “Numbers and percentages.” Its direction for millions and billions in body copy is explicit: “We express these numbers with a numeral and a word.” That supports 47.87 million km as English-language editorial practice. It gives no four-figure rule. NASA’s separate History Style Guide, under measurements, says “For metric measurements, use the International System of Units.” These are guides with identifiable audiences and purposes. 

International Astronomical Union and astronomy journals. The IAU Style Manual (1989), §2.41, asks that “the unit of tabulation is unambiguous.” The later and decisive source for the symbol is IAU Resolution B2 (2012), recommendations 1 and 5: “149 597 870 700 m exactly” and “that the unique symbol ‘au’ be used for the astronomical unit.” Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, author instructions under “Units,” lists “m, km, au, light-year, pc.” The American Astronomical Society’s machine-readable table standard likewise lists “au” for “astronomical unit.” I found no visitor-facing digit count in these journal guides. 

The Astronomical Almanac and NIST. The US Naval Observatory says its Explanatory Supplement covers the “sources, methods of computation, and use of the data” in the Almanac. I found no public-display rule there. NIST Special Publication 811 does give two applicable instructions. Its checklist calls for grouping digits with a “thin, fixed space.” Appendix B.7.2 asks that conversion respect the “maximum possible rounding error of the unconverted numerical value.” That second point matters when two units sit beside one another. 

Planetarium and science-museum associations. None found. I checked the International Planetarium Society’s education material, including The Value of the Planetarium in Education, and the Association of Science and Technology Centers’ exhibit-label research, including Watch Your Language! People Do Read Labels. Neither supplied a rule for units, significant figures or large-number formatting on a solar-system label.

The WorldWide Telescope User Manual, under the Finder Scope, describes its Distance entry as “the distance to the object in light years.” I found no Adler-published specification for how many digits a planet-to-planet distance should show, and no basis for assigning that Finder Scope convention to every Adler exhibit.

4. What should Paloma’s label look like?

a. Use “47.87 million km.” A visitor can see the scale and all four intended digits at a glance. 47,870,000 km has the same value, but its final zeros do two possible jobs: they can hold the place or report precision. A reader cannot tell which from the integer alone. 4.787 × 10⁷ km makes the four digits clear but asks the visitor to interpret an exponent. All three styles have reputable precedents. Hayden uses scale words in Appendix A and powers of ten in Appendix E. NASA Science’s Cosmic Distances uses a fully written “1,400,000,000-kilometer” distance in its Saturn example. 

b. Keep both units and change which one leads. For the Moon example I would print 384 400 km from Earth (0.002570 au). The initial zeros locate the decimal point. The four significant digits are 2, 5, 7, 0. A small nonzero distance can accidentally display as zero if the page fixes the number of decimal places. It will retain its first meaningful digit if the page counts significant figures. Leading with kilometres also spares the visitor from counting initial zeros. NASA’s Basics of Space Flight, Chapter 1, describes the Moon’s average distance as “about 384,400 km.” Its Cosmic Distances article expresses Saturn’s orbital scale as “9.5 AU.” These are precedents for choosing the unit by scale. Keeping the second unit visible is my recommendation for an orrery where visitors can change the centre and compare bodies. 

c. Convert first and round each display once. The Moon gives a particularly clear test. Starting with 384 400 km, the IAU conversion gives approximately 0.0025695553 au, which prints as 0.002570 au. Convert that printed au value back and you get approximately 384 467 km, which prints to four figures as 384 500 km. The original pair is sound. The apparent discrepancy arose because the intermediate rounding discarded information. Both labels should come from the same underlying distance, and the physical uncertainty should be converted into each unit before choosing its last place. NIST Appendix B.7.2 makes the same underlying point about preserving the information available in conversion. 

d. Use “au.” At first use, write astronomical unit (au), about 150 million km. Afterwards use au. NASA’s public Cosmic Distances article says “Astronomical units, abbreviated AU,” so a reader will meet both in the wild. The IAU resolution gives Paloma’s an explicit basis for choosing lowercase. 

e. For international readers, use a narrow nonbreaking space to group the integer: 384 400 km. It keeps the digits together on a phone and avoids giving the comma two jobs across languages. NIST’s §10.5.3 gives the reason: “the comma is widely used as the decimal marker outside the United States.” On the English page, billion is readable. Define it once in the units note as one thousand million. That preserves the readability of 25.80 billion km without asking international visitors to infer which scale the site means. 

f. Keep miles out of the distance box. There is a genuine familiarity benefit for some US visitors. Adler’s public example shows the case for miles, and NASA includes them in Voyager prose. I found no audience evidence showing that omitting miles materially harms comprehension on a display like Paloma’s. My judgment is that a third version of every distance costs more attention than it earns. If you want one bridge for US readers, put about 93 million miles in the first-use explanation of an au. NASA uses precisely that comparison. 

5. Can one source earn four digits for tracked spacecraft as a class?

None found for every tracked craft, date and selected centre. There is strong published evidence for excellent tracking performance, and there is a way to use it without leaving every spacecraft blank.

Thornton and Border’s Radiometric Tracking Techniques for Deep-Space Navigation, Chapter 3, §3.6, p. 34, gives the most relevant sentence: “Long arcs of Doppler and range data can, under favorable conditions, provide angular position accuracy of 40 nrad or better.” An arc is a span of tracking observations. Doppler measures motion toward or away from the station. A nanoradian is one billionth of a radian, a unit of angle. At one au, 40 nanoradians corresponds to about 6 km sideways, calculated from the quoted angle and the IAU unit. The qualifications matter: long arcs, favorable conditions, and an angular component. The sentence gives no universal three-sigma bound on distance from a selected planet. 

JPL’s presentation Radio Metric Orbit Determination Accuracy — Radial Versus Angular Components makes the distinction visible in its “1999 Capability” table. The position column gives “Radial Error” as “2 m” and “Angular Error (at 1 AU)” as “3 km.” Here radial is along the Earth–spacecraft line. A spacecraft can be very well located along that line while its sideways position is less certain. Near a moon, that sideways displacement can point almost directly toward the moon. The centre a visitor chooses therefore changes which error matters. 

For a trajectory fitted to tracking data, I would accept one published bound covering an identified mission segment and reference frame. It could earn distances throughout that segment wherever the bound passes the rounding test. A reliable bound on the full relative position also bounds the error in the distance between the same two centres. This avoids demanding a separate publication for every date. A concrete lead is Analysis of Cassini Altimetric Crossovers on Titan, in the orbital-error discussion and Table 3. The authors identify “the accuracy of the reconstructed ephemerides in the radial direction” relative to Titan. Their table also flags an important exception: “Orbital uncertainty set to an average value, not computed for given pass.” Before using it for Paloma’s, match the pass and the reconstructed trajectory used by the page. 

For an ended mission at last contact, keep a distance if the reconstruction and its accuracy evidence cover that instant. Ending radio contact does not erase the observations already made. The timestamp of the last transmission, by itself, supplies no position uncertainty. For a predicted path, use evidence covering the prediction date and how far ahead the trajectory was forecast. An accuracy estimate established over a fitted span cannot simply be carried forward. Thornton and Border’s §3.6, Figure 3-8, illustrates why even a small unmodelled motion can affect the inferred path. 

6. What would I change in the rule and style together?

Replace “one part in ten thousand” as an acceptance test with a test of the actual last place in each unit. Consider 25.80 billion km. Its last place is 10 million km, and half a place is 5 million km. For 172.5 au, half a place is 0.05 au, about 7.48 million km. A hypothetical 6 million km uncertainty is smaller than half a place in the au label and larger than half a place in the km label. Both labels have four significant figures. They make different demands on the evidence. If Tony wants the same number of figures in both, use the count supported by the more demanding unit. The conversion here uses the IAU’s exact definition. 

Carry the page’s own positioning error through to the label. Even where JPL’s position is excellent, Paloma’s may be drawing a point between saved positions. The project’s provenance rule already identifies “the working-out between served points.” A source that earns four figures at a stored point does not automatically earn them at every intervening minute. Also, apply the three-sigma rule only to a quantity for which the source gives a standard deviation, the statistical measure denoted by sigma. A tracking capability, a typical error and a maximum error each mean something different. Project provenance rule, “A Computed Position Prints What Its Errors Earn”⁠￼.

Give the visitor one short standing explanation. I suggest: “Distances are rounded to at most four significant figures for readability. We show fewer when the available position data support fewer.” In the box itself, keep the centre beside the number, especially for spacecraft. “From the centre of Titan” and “above Titan’s surface” answer different questions. When a distance is withheld, the project can still name the position source. That preserves Tony’s distinction between we can draw this path and we have evidence for these digits.
