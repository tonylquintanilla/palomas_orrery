## The terms, kept and explained

Your rulings: "Use hyperbolic because that's the scientific term and
the plain words to say what it means" (Oct 24), and the note above.

Each term is written as the term, a colon, then what it means. A
visitor reads one body's panel at a time, so each description explains
its own terms; the same sentence is used for the same term every time,
so a visitor who reads two learns it once.

| Term | Where | The sentence |
|---|---|---|
| hyperbolic | every comet JPL classes HYP or HYA | "Hyperbolic: its orbit is an open curve, not an ellipse like a planet's, so on its present path it does not loop back around the Sun." |
| near-parabolic | piece C of C/2025 K1 | "Near-parabolic: its orbit is so close to the line between an ellipse and an open curve that JPL's numbers cannot yet tell which it is." |
| retrograde | a tilt over 90 degrees in JPL's database | "Retrograde: it moves around the Sun in the opposite direction to the planets." |
| perihelion | wherever a description names the comet's closest pass | "perihelion, its closest pass by the Sun" |
| Oort cloud | wherever a description names it | "the Oort cloud, the far-off cloud of icy bodies thought to surround the solar system" |
| interstellar | the three in that group | "interstellar: it came from another star system" |
| predicted | the path's text box | JPL's word for where a path goes next, as the spacecraft's text box uses it |

"On its present path" is there on purpose. JPL's orbit is the one the
comet is on now; a comet from the Oort cloud can have its path changed
by the planets, so "never comes back" would claim more than JPL's
numbers do.

An aphelion (farthest point from the Sun) has no place in these
fourteen: an open curve has none. The slice 1 comets' orbit line already
gives perihelion and aphelion distances.

## A comet's text box

The same shape as a spacecraft's, three lines:

1. Its distance from the Sun, printed to the place its error earns, as
   for a spacecraft.
2. "JPL's own uncertainty for this position is not yet included." --
   printed while the comet's uncertainty is not served.
3. **"Its path is solid where it has already travelled, and dashed
   where JPL predicts it will go, as far as <the served path's last
   day>. It is drawn from a year before its perihelion to a year after
   today, or less where JPL's path stops sooner; those two years are
   our choice for the picture."**

"Predicted" is JPL's word, as for the spacecraft. "Travelled" rather
than the spacecraft's "flown". The second sentence is the span
ruling's: "The two margins are drawing choices; the hover says so."

Its source line, as a spacecraft's: JPL Horizons, with its address,
the Horizons id, the span drawn, the day retrieved and the step.

### Hyperbolic comets (10)

**Tsuchinshan** -- JPL: Tsuchinshan-ATLAS (C/2023 A3) -- tilt 139 deg
- Today: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. Tsuchinshan-ATLAS is a new comet
  discovered in 2023, expected to become bright in 2024.
- Proposed: Discovered in January 2023, it became known as the Great
  Comet of 2024, visible to the naked eye around its perihelion, its
  closest pass by the Sun, in late September 2024. Hyperbolic: its
  orbit is an open curve, not an ellipse like a planet's, so on its
  present path it does not loop back around the Sun. Retrograde: it
  moves around the Sun in the opposite direction to the planets.
- Link: Wikipedia, "C/2023 A3 (Tsuchinshan-ATLAS)" (unchanged).

**ATLAS** -- JPL: ATLAS (C/2024 G3) -- tilt 117 deg
- Today: Retrograde. Hyperbolic. The Great Comet of 2025. Comet C/2024
  G3 (ATLAS) created quite a buzz in the Southern Hemisphere!
- Proposed: Discovered in April 2024 and called the Great Comet of
  2025, the brightest comet of that year. After its perihelion, its
  closest pass by the Sun, it was clearly visible to the naked eye
  from the Southern Hemisphere. Hyperbolic: its orbit is an open curve,
  not an ellipse like a planet's, so on its present path it does not
  loop back around the Sun. Retrograde: it moves around the Sun in the
  opposite direction to the planets.
- Link: Wikipedia, "C/2024 G3 (ATLAS)" (unchanged).

**McNaught** -- JPL: McNaught (C/2006 P1) -- tilt 77.8 deg
- Today: Hyperbolic. Known as the Great Comet of 2007. Retrograde

- Proposed: Discovered in August 2006 by Robert McNaught and called the

## What stays with the build, by method

- Each entry is keyed in the orrery in the same round as its website
  entry, with JPL's exact name, as the spacecraft were.
- The path plan plans each comet by the span ruling: a year before its
  perihelion, found from JPL's positions, to a year after today, both
  ends clipped to what JPL serves; the perihelion is the plan's one
  event.
- A comet keeps the orrery's colour, and its marker the orrery's
  diamond.
- No orbit line and no period line for an open path, as for a
  spacecraft.
- The rule your note sets, to keep the term and explain it, goes into
  interactive-exhibit's Hover text is written for the visitor at its
  next version.
