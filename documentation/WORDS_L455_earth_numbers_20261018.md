## 1. Where a source changes what a visitor learns

These follow from the sources by rule. They come first because each
changes a fact on the page.

1. **The inner core: "near 5,400 degrees Celsius" becomes "up to about
   5,600 kelvin".** From NASA's Scientific Visualization Studio, "A Guide
   to Cosmic Temperatures" (2023), which prints the figure in kelvin.
   NASA's "Facts About Earth" gives 5,400 degrees Celsius, which is
   5,700 kelvin; that is recorded on the row. A 2013 laboratory result
   (Anzellini et al., reported by the ESRF) is hotter, about 6,000
   degrees Celsius, also recorded on the row.
2. **The crust: "5 to 10 km under the oceans ... 30 to 50 km under the
   continents" becomes "about 5 km under the oceans ... about 30 km on
   average under land".** NASA's "Facts About Earth" gives those two
   figures. No open page carrying the two ranges was found.
3. **The surface temperatures: "about minus 80 to 60 degrees Celsius"
   becomes the two records.** "The coldest temperature measured at the
   surface is 184.0 kelvin, at Vostok in Antarctica, and the hottest is
   329.8 kelvin, at Furnace Creek in California." From the World
   Meteorological Organization's table of records: -89.2 and 56.7
   degrees Celsius. This settles the ledger's question about converting
   "minus 80 to 60": that range had no source, and the records do.
4. **The troposphere's temperatures: "about 15 degrees Celsius at sea
   level to minus 60" becomes "about 290 kelvin at the surface to about
   222 kelvin".** NOAA JetStream, the page the stratosphere's height
   already comes from, gives an average of 17 degrees Celsius falling to
   -51 at the tropopause. The old "minus 60" was the page's Fahrenheit
   figure written as Celsius.
5. **The thermosphere: "2,000 degrees Celsius" becomes "about 2,000
   kelvin".** NOAA's 2,000 degrees Celsius is 2,273 kelvin. The page
   gives it to the thousand, so it stays 2,000 at that place.
6. **The low orbit's period: "about 90 to 120 minutes" becomes "about
   88 to 127 minutes".** Worked out, not typed: a circular orbit at each
   edge, by Kepler's third law, from the two edges' rows and Earth's
   gravitational constant. NASA's Hubble fact sheet gives about 95
   minutes for Hubble, which sits inside the range.

## 2. Brought back with a source, and what is still out

### Back, each from a page read today

- **The outer core: "from about 4,100 kelvin where it meets the mantle
  to about 5,600 kelvin where it meets the inner core".** Both from
  Andrault and others (2016), "The deep Earth may not be cooling down",
  Earth and Planetary Science Letters, open full text: 4,100 +/- 200 K at
  the core-mantle boundary and 5,580 +/- 600 K at the inner core's
  boundary. Each prints to the place its uncertainty allows. The 5,600
  at the inner boundary agrees with the inner core's own 5,600 kelvin
  from NASA. (Revision 2's 4,800 to 5,800 was National Geographic's.)
- **The mantle, split again into its two layers.** The upper mantle:
  "about 1,650 kelvin near its top and about 1,990 kelvin at its base".
  The lower mantle: "from about 1,960 kelvin at its top to about 4,100
  kelvin where it meets the core". The 1,650, 1,990 and 1,960 are from
  Katsura (2022), "A Revised Adiabatic Temperature Profile for the Mantle",
  J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth, open full text (1,646 K at 50 km depth,
  1,994 K at the 660 km boundary's top and 1,960 K at its bottom); the
  4,100 is Andrault's, as above. The old words' 500 to 2,200 and 2,200
  to 4,500 degrees Celsius are gone; Katsura's profile is hundreds of
  kelvin cooler at the boundary. (Revision 2's 1,000 and 4,000 were
  National Geographic's.)
- **The atmosphere's mass: "Together they hold more than 99 percent of
  the atmosphere's mass".** The old words' figure, now with a source:
  UCAR Center for Science Education, "Demonstrating the Thickness of
  Atmospheric Layers": "over 99 percent of the mass of Earth's
  atmosphere is in its two lowest layers, the troposphere and the
  stratosphere." (Revision 2's 98 percent was National Geographic's.)
- **The thermopause: a note on the upper atmosphere, "Drawn at a nominal
  top of 600 km. The real top, the thermopause, lies anywhere between
  about 500 and 1,000 km, rising and falling with the energy coming from
  the Sun."** UCAR Center for Science Education.
- **A storm's compression, now in the magnetopause's about: "In a strong
  storm the wind can push it inside the geostationary ring: on 21 January
  2005 its nose came within about 3 Earth radii of Earth's centre."**
  Dmitriev and others (2014), J. Geophys. Res., open on arXiv. The old
  note's "6.6 R_E" is the ring's own radius, which its hover prints.
- **The Moon in the Hill sphere: "The Moon, about 60.27 Earth radii away,
  lies deep within it."** NASA's Moon Fact Sheet, 384,400 km, printed in
  Earth radii at its count.
- **The magnetotail's three sizes, back in its about and note** (about
  120 Earth radii, about 60 across, out to 220), now printed from their
  rows.
- **The outer edge of low orbit: "about 2,000 km up" is back** in its
  description, from its row.
- **The orrery's low orbit: the residents and the counts are back.** The
  International Space Station 370 to 460 km up, inclined 51.6 degrees
  (ESA); Hubble about 550 km up (NASA's fact sheet says about 340 miles);
  Starlink, 11,140 satellites working (Jonathan McDowell's catalogue,
  15 October 2026), most near 550 km (NASA Orbital Debris Quarterly News,
  September 2025); about 15,900 satellites still working in all orbits
  (ESA, 14 October 2026) and 28,015 objects tracked and catalogued in low
  Earth orbit (ESA's table of orbit regimes, same page and date). The old
  figures were 400, 540, 7,000, 11,000 "in LEO" and 35,000, typed from
  sources nobody here had read. ESA's own count for low orbit is 28,015,
  not 35,000. Counts this size change every month; the dates sit in the
  hover's source lines.
- **Hubble's height.** Gemini could not open NASA's fact sheet (its
  reader was blocked from the PDF), but found the same "about 340 miles"
  on two other NASA pages. It was opened here before, so it stays.

### Still out, and why

- **The six minutes a satellite takes to cross the sky.** Still no
  source. Gemini found an ESA page for children that says "several
  minutes", and NASA pages giving about five minutes and three minutes
  for particular passes of the Station. Passes differ, so there is no
  single figure to print, and "several minutes" is too loose to be worth
  a sentence.
- **"5x farther out" for the geostationary ring.** Both distances print
  side by side in the hover. A ratio worked from the two rows would print
  to seven figures, 5.032690.
- **The heights in the two low-orbit names**, "(180 km)" and "(2,000
  km)". A name cannot yet be filled from a row, which is the same case as
  the Sun's chromosphere name on L-411. The hover's next lines print both
  heights from their rows.
- **35,786 km in the geostationary ring's description.** The hover
  prints the height on its next line. A row worked from the store gives
  35,786.04 km, while the page's own subtraction of the rounded radius
  prints 35,786.03, so the two would disagree in the last digit; that is
  recorded on L-455 to settle with the ring's hover.
- **The magnetopause model's "alpha is 0.59" and "a6 is 0.58 +/- 0.01".**
  The model's sentences in the i panel are not filled from rows, and
  alpha has no row of its own.
- **Dates of rulings, ledger handles, "figure 30" and "L* = 4.5" in the
  belts' and the lower atmosphere's notes.** These were our bookkeeping,
  not facts about Earth.
- **The crust's note on its citation** ("Prsa et al. (2016) ... IAU 2015
  Resolution B3"). It is wording about a citation, which belongs on the
  row in the store.

## 4. A rule the new check holds: dates

The store keeps no dates as rows. So a date of an event typed in a
served word stands only where the work that states it is named where the
visitor reads it, on the feature's Source line or in the sentence. Three
cases on Earth, all kept:

- "In April 2029 the asteroid Apophis will pass about 32,000 km above
  Earth's surface" -- NASA's "Apophis Facts" now sits on the ring's
  Source line, brought there by the 32,000 km, which is a row.
- "in 2020 was tilted ... (IGRF-13 model)" in the belts' plane sentence
  -- the model the tilt comes from is named in the sentence, and 2020 is
  its epoch.

- "on 21 January 2005" in the magnetopause's storm sentence -- Dmitriev
  and others (2014) sits on the magnetopause's Source line, brought there
  by the 3 Earth radii, which is a row.

The check lists each with its work and fails if the work goes missing.
